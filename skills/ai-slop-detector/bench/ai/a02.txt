Beneath the surface of our oceans lies one of the most breathtaking and vital ecosystems on the planet: coral reefs. Often referred to as the "rainforests of the sea," these vibrant underwater worlds are home to an astonishing diversity of life, from tiny shrimp to majestic sea turtles.

Coral reefs are built by tiny animals called coral polyps, which secrete calcium carbonate to form hard skeletons. Over thousands of years, these skeletons accumulate to create the intricate structures we see today. What makes this process truly remarkable is the symbiotic relationship between corals and microscopic algae known as zooxanthellae. These algae live within the coral's tissues, providing them with nutrients through photosynthesis while also giving reefs their stunning colors.

But coral reefs are more than just beautiful. They play a pivotal role in supporting marine biodiversity, protecting coastlines from erosion, and sustaining the livelihoods of millions of people who depend on fishing and tourism. In fact, although reefs cover less than one percent of the ocean floor, they support roughly a quarter of all marine species.

Sadly, these delicate ecosystems are facing unprecedented threats. Rising ocean temperatures, ocean acidification, pollution, and overfishing are all taking a toll. Coral bleaching, which occurs when stressed corals expel their algae, has become increasingly common in recent decades.

The good news is that there is still hope. Through conservation efforts, sustainable practices, and global cooperation, we can help protect these underwater treasures for generations to come. After all, the health of our coral reefs is deeply intertwined with the health of our planet.
