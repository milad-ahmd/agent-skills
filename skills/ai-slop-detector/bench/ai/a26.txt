Mount St. Helens is a stratovolcano in southwestern Washington, part of the Cascade Volcanic Arc that stretches from northern California to British Columbia. The arc exists because the Juan de Fuca Plate is sliding beneath the North American Plate, and as the descending slab heats up, it releases water that lowers the melting point of the overlying mantle and generates magma.

Compared with neighbors like Mount Rainier or Mount Adams, Mount St. Helens is geologically young. Most of the cone visible before 1980 was built within the last few thousand years, through alternating eruptions of sticky dacite lava, ash, and pyroclastic flows, along with occasional andesite and basalt.

The 1980 eruption reshaped the volcano dramatically. The collapse of the north flank removed roughly 400 meters from the summit and left a horseshoe-shaped crater open to the north. Since then, the volcano has continued to evolve. Between 1980 and 1986, a series of eruptions built a lava dome inside the crater, and from 2004 to 2008 a second period of dome growth added a large volume of new rock.

Scientists at the USGS Cascades Volcano Observatory continue to monitor the volcano using seismometers, GPS stations, and gas measurements. The data suggest that magma is slowly recharging the system, which is not unusual for an active volcano. While another eruption is likely at some point, there are currently no signs that one is imminent.

Overall, Mount St. Helens offers a valuable window into how volcanoes in subduction zones grow, collapse, and rebuild over time.
