Mount St. Helens, located in Washington State's Cascade Range, is one of the most famous volcanoes in the United States. On May 18, 1980, it erupted in what remains the deadliest and most economically destructive volcanic event in U.S. history.

In the weeks leading up to the eruption, the volcano showed clear signs of activity. A series of earthquakes and steam explosions signaled that magma was rising beneath the surface, and the north flank of the mountain began to bulge outward at an alarming rate of up to five feet per day.

At 8:32 a.m. on May 18, a magnitude 5.1 earthquake triggered a massive landslide on the north face, the largest in recorded history. This sudden release of pressure unleashed a lateral blast that flattened forests over an area of roughly 230 square miles. A towering column of ash rose more than 15 miles into the atmosphere, and ash fell across eleven states.

The eruption claimed 57 lives, destroyed hundreds of homes, and reduced the mountain's height by about 1,300 feet. Yet in the decades since, Mount St. Helens has become a living laboratory for scientists studying ecological recovery. Plants and animals have gradually returned to the blast zone, offering valuable insights into how ecosystems rebuild after catastrophic disturbance.

Today, the Mount St. Helens National Volcanic Monument stands as a powerful reminder of nature's raw power and resilience, attracting visitors and researchers from around the world.
